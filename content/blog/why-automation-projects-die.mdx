Every month, mid-sized companies spend substantial capital buying software, subscribing to workflow tools, and hiring agencies to automate their operations. Three months later, the spreadsheets are back on desktop screens, purchase orders are being approved via WhatsApp screenshots, and the new system is an abandoned ghost town.

Leadership usually blames the team: *"Our staff is resistant to change."*

That diagnosis is almost always wrong. People do not resist change because they enjoy manual data entry. They resist new systems because the new system makes their daily job harder than the old shortcut.

## The Vendor Demo Trap

Most enterprise software is purchased by executives who will never log into the software to do actual daily work. 

During the sales presentation, the demo looks spotless. Beautiful pie charts update in real time. Drill-down reports export with one click. The executive signs the contract thinking, *"Finally, we will have total visibility over our operations."*

What nobody notices during the demo is the hidden input cost. For that executive dashboard to show accurate data, a site engineer standing in 40-degree heat must open an app, navigate four submenus, select six mandatory dropdown fields, and manually type invoice reference numbers on a small smartphone screen.

If the app takes 90 seconds to submit an update, and sending a photo on WhatsApp takes four seconds, WhatsApp wins every single time. Within three weeks, the field stops updating the system. The dashboard shows stale data. Leadership stops looking at it. The project is dead.

## Automating a Broken Process Breaks It Faster

The second fatal mistake is attempting to automate a workflow that is already messy in real life.

If your company does not have a clear rule for who signs off on an unplanned purchase when the plant manager is traveling, writing an automated rule for purchase approvals will not fix the ambiguity. It will simply trap the purchase request in an automated bottleneck.

Automation does not create discipline; it enforces existing discipline. Before you automate a single step, you must map the physical reality of how work moves:

- Who actually makes the decision?
- What minimum information do they require to say yes or no?
- What happens if someone does not respond within four hours?

If you cannot write the rules down clearly on a single sheet of paper using plain language, you are not ready to automate it in software.

## The Three Rules of Systems That Stick

Over the past decade—first running my own agency and now leading IT and digitalisation inside an operating EPC business—I have found that systems survive only when they follow three strict rules:

### 1. The input must be easier than the shortcut
If your new workflow requires more keystrokes, more screen transitions, or more cognitive effort than sending a voice note on WhatsApp, nobody will use it when things get busy. 

Design the input layer around the user's physical environment. For field teams, that means simple mobile web forms with pre-filled fields, automated camera capture, and single-click buttons. 

### 2. The person doing the data entry must get immediate value
Most systems fail because they extract effort from junior staff solely to give reports to senior directors. The person typing the data gets zero benefit; they only get more administrative burden.

Turn that equation around. If an administrative coordinator keys in a dispatch update, the system should immediately generate the delivery note, email the transporter, and update their daily tracking sheet automatically. When the system saves the user personal time, they protect the system.

### 3. Build in the open with the actual operators
Never design an operational workflow behind closed doors in a conference room. 

Sit next to the billing clerk for two hours on Monday morning. Watch where they copy numbers from one window to another. Ask them which part of their week causes them the most frustration. When you build tools that eliminate the exact tasks your staff hates doing, you will not have to push change management—they will pull the system into production themselves.

## The Bottom Line

Digital transformation is not a technology purchase. It is an operational agreement between leadership and front-line staff. If you want automation that lasts past the 90-day mark, stop looking at vendor feature lists and start looking at how your team actually gets through their workday.
