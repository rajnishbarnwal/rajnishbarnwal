If you manage operations at a growing company in India, you have almost certainly sat through an HR software sales pitch. 

The vendor presents a slick slide deck showing biometric integrations, mobile leave applications, automated tax deductions, and employee performance tracking. On paper, it sounds like the solution to every administrative headache.

Then, six months post-purchase, you discover that field staff still record attendance on physical registers, the accounts team still calculates payroll in Excel because the software cannot handle custom site allowances, and you are paying an annual bill for features nobody touches.

Before signing an annual HRMS contract, ask the vendor these six practical questions.

## 1. What is the real total cost of ownership at 100, 250, and 500 users?

HRMS pricing is frequently structured on a per-user per-month (PUPM) basis. When you evaluate the software with 40 head-office employees, ₹150 per user per month looks affordable.

However, in companies with large field forces, technicians, storekeepers, or site staff, your employee headcount is significantly higher. Paying full per-seat rates for a site worker who only logs attendance twice a day makes little economic sense.

Ask for tiered pricing models, ask whether read-only or attendance-only user types exist, and calculate what the renewal invoice will look like in year three when your headcount doubles.

## 2. How easily can we export ALL our data if we decide to leave?

Vendors make it very easy to import data into their platform. They make it considerably harder to extract it.

Ensure that the vendor provides automated, unthrottled exports of historical attendance logs, leave balances, salary revision archives, and uploaded compliance documents in open formats like CSV or SQL dumps. If getting your own company records out requires paying "custom professional service fees," look elsewhere.

## 3. How does the system handle non-standard Indian operating realities?

Generic HR tools are frequently built around standard corporate desk jobs with 9-to-6 office hours. Mid-sized industrial and contracting firms operate under completely different constraints:

- Site allowances that vary based on project geography and climatic conditions.
- Staggered weekly offs and rotational project shifts.
- Temporary contractual staffing and sub-contractor muster rolls.

Ask the sales representative to configure one of your actual messy site attendance scenarios live on the call, without using pre-scripted demo templates.

## 4. Are REST APIs included in the base plan, or locked behind an enterprise tier?

Your HRMS cannot live on an island. It must communicate with your core accounting package, your internal project costing models, and your communication channels.

Many vendors market API connectivity, only for you to discover during implementation that API access is restricted to an "Enterprise Plan" costing double the base subscription, or limited by restrictive daily request quotas. Demand written confirmation on API availability and rate limits.

## 5. What is the mobile app experience on low-end smartphones with poor network connectivity?

Site supervisors and field teams do not carry high-end devices with high-speed 5G connectivity.

If the mobile app takes 15 seconds to boot, requires 100MB of storage, or crashes when network connectivity drops in remote locations, field teams will abandon it immediately. Test the mobile interface on an entry-level smartphone under throttled network conditions.

## 6. Should we build a focused internal tool instead?

For many mid-sized companies, 80% of administrative friction comes from just three requirements:
- Reliable, tamper-proof attendance logging.
- Clear leave approvals.
- Accurate payroll data exports for accounts.

If your core needs are focused, building a lean, custom internal application using modern frameworks like Next.js and PostgreSQL often delivers better adoption at a fraction of ongoing SaaS licensing fees. 

You get full control over workflow logic, zero per-seat licensing penalties as headcount grows, and an interface built exactly around how your business operates.

## Conclusion

Software should adapt to your operational structure—not the other way around. Ask tough questions before you commit your budget, and prioritize adoption simplicity over exhaustive feature lists.
